Abstract and Figures

The job demands–resources (JD-R) model proposes that working conditions can be categorized into 2 broad categories, job demands and job resources, that are differentially related to specific outcomes. A series of LISREL analyses using self-reports as well as observer ratings of the working conditions provided strong evidence for the JD-R model: Job demands are primarily related to the exhaustion component of burnout, whereas (lack of) job resources are primarily related to disengagement. Highly similar patterns were observed in each of 3 occupational groups: human services, industry, and transport (total N = 374). In addition, results confirmed the 2-factor structure (exhaustion and disengagement) of a new burnout instrument—the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory—and suggested that this structure is essentially invariant across occupational groups.

Different types of human–AI interaction and employees’ knowledge territorial behavior: the role of organizational dehumanization and paradoxical leadership
Article
Full-text available
  • Jul 2026
Introduction As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly embedded in organizational workflows, understanding how human–AI interactions shape employees’ knowledge territorial behavior has become a critical issue in digital transformation. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, this study examines the differential effects of enhanced and impeded human–AI interaction on employees’ knowledge territorial behavior, with organizational dehumanization as the underlying psychological mechanism and paradoxical leadership as a boundary condition. Methods We tested the hypotheses across two studies. Study 1 used a scenario-based experiment to establish the causal effects of different forms of human–AI interaction. Study 2 employed a two-wave field survey to validate the full moderated mediation model in a real-world organizational context. Results Enhanced human–AI interaction was negatively associated with knowledge territorial behavior, whereas impeded human–AI interaction was positively associated with such behavior. Organizational dehumanization significantly mediated these relationships. In addition, paradoxical leadership moderated these effects: higher levels of paradoxical leadership strengthened the negative effect of enhanced human–AI interaction on organizational dehumanization and attenuated the positive effect of impeded human–AI interaction on organizational dehumanization. Discussion These findings reveal the double-edged effects of human–AI interaction on employees’ knowledge territorial behavior. This research extends the literature on the antecedents of knowledge territoriality, highlights organizational dehumanization as a key psychological mechanism, and identifies paradoxical leadership as an effective managerial approach for facilitating human–AI collaboration in modern organizations.

A moderated mediation role of job crafting in the pathway from online workload through technostress to burnout
Article
Full-text available
  • Jul 2026
With technology-driven work patterns persisting beyond COVID-19, employee technostress and burnout remain rising concerns. This study examines whether job crafting continues to buffer the online workload–technostress–burnout process in this altered context. Using an online survey, data were collected from 688 employees in Thailand working remotely at least twice a week, recruited through convenience sampling. Path analysis revealed that technostress fully mediated the relationship between online workload and burnout. While job crafting did not significantly reduce technostress, it directly lowered burnout and weakened the indirect impact of online workload on burnout via technostress. These findings suggest that job crafting is not universally effective across all digital stressors but may mitigate burnout when applied to manageable demands, such as online workload. The study underscores technostress as a central mechanism linking digital demands to employee strain and highlights job crafting’s conditional role in promoting resilience in technology-driven work environments.

Job Autonomy, Organizational Culture, and Employee Creativity as Predictors of Innovative Work Behavior: The Mediating Role of Knowledge Sharing at Bank Indonesia
Article
Full-text available
  • Jul 2026
This study examines the determinants of Innovation Work Behavior (IWB) among 150 employees of Bank Indonesia's Compliance Management and Reporting Department (DPKL), using PLS-SEM with Knowledge Sharing as a mediator. Employee Creativity and Knowledge Sharing significantly predict IWB, while Organizational Culture affects IWB indirectly through Knowledge Sharing. Job Autonomy shows no significant effect on either Knowledge Sharing or IWB, reflecting the constraints of a highly regulated environment. The model explains 80.2% of IWB variance. This study contributes by testing an integrated antecedent-mediator model in an underexplored central-bank compliance setting. Keywords: Job Autonomy; Organizational Culture; Employee Creativity; Knowledge Sharing; Innovation Work Behavior; PLS-SEM

Determination of Burnout Through Work Life Balance, Job Stress, and Digital Stress in Private University Lecturers
Article
Full-text available
  • Dec 2025
The acceleration of digitalization in higher education has increased work demands on lecturers, particularly through technology-based administration, continuous information flow, and expectations of rapid responsiveness. This study examines the effects of work-life balance, job stress, and digital stress on burnout and their impact on job satisfaction among private university lecturers in the LLDIKTI X region. Grounded in the Job Demands-Resources model, this research adopts a quantitative approach using Structural Equation Modeling (PLS) based on survey data collected from lecturers. The results show that job stress and digital stress significantly increase burnout, while work-life balance is also associated with higher burnout in the context of blurred work–life boundaries. Job stress reduces job satisfaction, whereas digital stress exhibits a dual effect by increasing both burnout and job satisfaction, indicating its role as both a hindrance and challenge demand. Burnout is identified as a key mediating variable linking work pressures to job satisfaction. Substantively, higher burnout leads to lower job satisfaction, confirming its central role in the health impairment process. This study contributes to the literature by extending the Job Demands-Resources model through the inclusion of digital stress as a contemporary work demand and by highlighting the complexity of lecturer well-being in the digital academic environment. The findings provide important implications for university management in designing policies that balance digital demands, support lecturer well-being, and sustain academic performance.

Work-Related Stress in the Private Sector: Causes, Consequences, and Coping Strategies
Article
  • Jul 2026
  • Beyza Erer
This study aims to comprehensively examine the key determinants and multidimensional outcomes of work-related stress among employees in private-sector organizations, with the objective of generating practical and sustainable strategic implications for both employees and employers. Adopting a qualitative approach grounded in a phenomenological research design, the study explores employees’ lived experiences of workplace stress and the meanings they attribute to these experiences. Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 30 private-sector employees and analyzed using thematic analysis.The findings reveal that work-related stress primarily stems from managerial inadequacies, job insecurity, and deficiencies in organizational communication. Participants frequently identified unfair managerial practices, fear of dismissal, micromanagement, toxic work environments, and non-transparent communication as major stressors adversely affecting psychological well-being and job satisfaction. Work-related stress was found to generate multidimensional consequences, including loss of motivation, emotional exhaustion, decreased job satisfaction, and heightened turnover intentions, thereby producing effects at both individual and organizational levels. Although participants reported employing individual and social coping strategies—such as time management, seeking social support, maintaining physical health, and striving for emotional balance—these efforts were perceived as insufficient in the absence of organizational support.Moreover, the findings indicate that organizational culture and leadership style play a critical role in shaping the intensity and persistence of stress experiences. Supportive and participative managerial approaches were identified as particularly influential in mitigating stress levels. Overall, the study demonstrates that work-related stress is not merely an individual or sector-specific issue but a multidimensional and structural phenomenon, underscoring the necessity of preventive, systemic, and sustainable organizational interventions.

Burnout as mediator between job demands and mental health in interpreters
Article
  • Jul 2026
  • J DEAF STUD DEAF EDU
This study explored burnout as a mediator between job demands and mental health among Filipino sign language interpreters. Using data from 83 participants, parallel mediation analyses tested whether intrapersonal and interpersonal demands were associated with anxiety and depression through personal and work-related burnout, while controlling for age. Results indicated that intrapersonal demands were positively associated with personal burnout, which in turn was associated with higher anxiety and depression. In contrast, work-related burnout did not significantly mediate any pathways, and interpersonal demands did not significantly associate with burnout or mental health outcomes. Age was negatively associated with both burnout types and indirectly influenced anxiety and depression through reduced personal burnout. These findings identify personal burnout as a key mechanism linking psychological job demands to mental health and highlight the importance of culturally informed approaches to supporting interpreter wellbeing.

Organizational embeddedness as a double-edged sword for avoidance crafting: a U-shaped relationship moderated by intrinsic motivation
Article
  • Jul 2026
Purpose This study aims to examine the curvilinear relationship between organizational embeddedness and avoidance crafting. We propose that both low and high levels of organizational embeddedness are associated with increased avoidance crafting, resulting in a U-shaped relationship. Additionally, the study explores intrinsic motivation as a key individual factor that may moderate the hypothesized U-shaped relationship. Design/methodology/approach Data were collected online from 270 USA employees at 2 time points. Participants reported organizational embeddedness, intrinsic motivation (Time 1) and avoidance crafting (Time 2). Polynomial and moderated regression analyses were used to test the U-shaped relationship and the moderation effect. Findings The findings support our hypotheses, revealing a significant U-shaped relationship between organizational embeddedness and avoidance crafting. Specifically, employees with either low or high levels of embeddedness were more likely to engage in avoidance crafting. Furthermore, this curvilinear relationship was particularly strong among employees with higher intrinsic motivation. Practical implications Managers should foster organizational embeddedness cautiously, as excessive levels may lead to avoidance crafting due to overburdened demands and reduced autonomy. Supporting employees' intrinsic motivation by enhancing autonomy, competence and relatedness can mitigate these risks. Regularly assessing embeddedness and intrinsic motivation allows managers to identify and address early signs of over-embeddedness or declining motivation, ensuring a balance that promotes well-being and engagement. Originality/value This study challenges the previously established linear positive relationship between organizational embeddedness and individual outcomes. It also contributes to understanding the interplay between individual and organizational factors in shaping work behaviors.

On the Meaning of Maslach's Three Dimensions of Burnout
Article
Full-text available
  • Dec 1990
  • Raymond T. Lee
  • Blake E Ashforth
The dimensionality of Maslach's (1982) 3 aspects of job burnout—emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment—was examined among a sample of supervisors and managers in the human services. A series of confirmatory factor analyses supported the 3-factor model, with the first 2 aspects highly correlated. The 3 aspects were found to be differentially related to other variables reflecting aspects of strain, stress coping, and self-efficacy in predictable and meaningful ways. Implications for better understanding the burnout process are discussed.

A Meta-Analytic Examination of the Correlates of the Three Dimensions of Job Burnout
Article
Full-text available
  • Apr 1996
  • Raymond T. Lee
  • Blake E Ashforth
This meta-analysis examined how demand and resource correlates and behavioral and attitudinal correlates were related to each of the 3 dimensions of job burnout. Both the demand and resource correlates were more strongly related to emotional exhaustion than to either depersonalization or personal accomplishment. Consistent with the conservation of resources theory of stress, emotional exhaustion was more strongly related to the demand correlates than to the resource correlates, suggesting that workers might have been sensitive to the possibility of resource loss. The 3 burnout dimensions were differentially related to turnover intentions, organizational commitment, and control coping. Implications for research and the amelioration of burnout are discussed.

Patient demands, lack of reciprocity, and burnout: a five‐year longitudinal study among general practitioners
Article
  • Jun 2000
  • J ORGAN BEHAV
  • Arnold B. Bakker
  • Wilmar B Schaufeli
  • H. Sixma
  • Dirk van Dierendonck
This study among a sample of 207 general practitioners (GPs) uses a five‐year longitudinal design to test a process model of burnout. On the basis of social exchange and equity theory, it is hypothesized and found that demanding patient contacts produce a lack of reciprocity in the GP–patient relationship, which, in turn, depletes GPs' emotional resources and initiates the burnout syndrome. More specifically, structural equation analyses confirmed that—both at T1 and T2—lack of reciprocity mediates the impact of patient demands on emotional exhaustion. Emotional exhaustion, in turn, evokes negative attitudes toward patients (depersonalization), and toward oneself in relation to the job (reduced personal accomplishment). Moreover, this process model of burnout was confirmed at T2, even after controlling for T1‐scores on each of the model components. Finally, T1 depersonalization predicted the intensity and frequency of T2 patient demands, after controlling for T1 patient demands. This major finding suggests that GPs who attempt to gain emotional distance from their patients as a way of coping with their exhaustion, evoke demanding and threatening patient behaviors themselves. The theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed. Copyright © 2000 John Wiley & Sons, Ltd.

The project brought together researchers from 9 EU-Countries and resulted in a number of actions, in particular the following: (a) There is an urgent need of defining the concept of flexible working hours, since it has been used in many different and even counterintuitive ways; the most obvious distinction is where the influence over the working hours lies, that is between the "company-based ... [Show full abstract] flexibility" and the "individual-oriented flexibility"; (b) The review of the Legislation in force in the 15 European countries shows that the regulation of working times is quite extensive and covers (Council Directive 93/104/EC) almost all the various arrangements of working hours (i.e., part-time, overtime, shift, and night work), but fails to provide for flexibility; (c) According to the data of the Third EU Survey on Working Conditions, longer and "irregular" working hours are in general linked to lower levels of health and well-being; moreover, low (individual) flexibility and high variability of working hours (i.e., company-based flexibility) were consistently associated with poor health and well-being, while low variability combined with high autonomy showed positive effects; (d) Six substudies from different countries demonstrated that flexible working hours vary according to country, economic sector, social status, and gender; overtime is the most frequent form of company-based flexibility but has negative effects on stress, sleep, and social and mental health; individual flexibility alleviates the negative effects of the company-based flexibility on subjective health, safety, and social well-being; (e) The literature review was able to list more than 1,000 references, but it was striking that most of these documents were mainly argumentative with very little empirical data. Consequently, there is a strong need for systematic research and well-controlled actions in order to examine in detail what flexible working hours are considered, what and where are their positive effects, in particular, as concerns autonomy, and what regulation seem most reasonable.
